I came to language neuroscience through education, then learned to connect questions about language with evidence from behavior, brain networks, and computational representations.

My path: from teaching to research

My undergraduate training was in language education, and my experience as a teacher was where my scientific questions about language first took shape. Working with students made language feel both practical and deeply mysterious: something people learn, adapt, and use with remarkable flexibility. It also continues to shape how I think about individual differences and the relationship between research and learning.

I pursued an M.S. in Neuroscience and Education at Teachers College, Columbia University, where studying how the nervous system implements sensation and action led me to ask how language interfaces with those systems.

The questions that emerged still guide me. What neural representations count as usable linguistic input? How are they built incrementally from noisy sensory signals? How do language networks interact with perceptual and motor circuits during comprehension, production, and inner speech?

Learning to connect theory and data

At Stanford, I developed these questions through work in the Laboratory for Computation and Language in the Mind and Brain. Surprisal and noisy-channel theories taught me to view comprehension as probabilistic inference: people combine incoming language with prior expectations and revise an interpretation as new evidence arrives.

A self-paced reading project on sentence repetition made this approach concrete. Repetition increased agreement in reading-time patterns across participants, but that observation alone could reflect either stronger stimulus-driven structure or adaptation. I reframed the ambiguity as a decoding problem and used cross-validated classification to test whether sentence identity became more recoverable. That experience shaped how I approach research now: translate a conceptual question into competing empirical signatures, then choose an analysis that can distinguish them.

Representations, networks, and communication

Training in machine learning and high-dimensional representations gave me another way to think about neural data. Representational similarity analysis makes it possible to ask how words, actions, or task states are organized in distributed population codes rather than reducing each condition to a single activation value. I now pair that representational perspective with connectivity analyses that ask how information is coordinated across regions over time.

My collaboration with Stanford’s Neural Prosthetics Translational Lab brought these questions into contact with speech restoration. Working with intensive fMRI data for language and speech–motor mapping convinced me that progress in brain-to-speech technology depends not only on better hardware and decoders, but also on a deeper account of how linguistic, sensory, and motor systems interact.

Current work

I am currently a lab manager in Stanford University’s Department of Linguistics, working with Dr. Cory Shain. I bring together experimental design, behavioral and neuroimaging data, and computational analysis to study language as a dynamic biological system. Across projects, I value careful operationalization, transparent analysis, and figures that make complex results easier to inspect and question.
